Our Thesis
The problem with Hormuz isn't geography.
It's decision latency.

We've studied every major energy chokepoint disruption since the 1967 Suez closure. The pattern is consistent: the companies that survive aren't the ones with the most reserves. They're the ones that make the right decisions in the first 72 hours.

During the 1984 Tanker War, Iranian attacks on shipping in the Gulf caused a 25% spike in marine insurance premiums overnight. Yet the price of crude barely moved for the first week — because the market couldn't process the signal. The same pattern emerged in September 2019, when drone strikes on Abqaiq-Khurais knocked out 5.7 million barrels per day of Saudi capacity. Brent spiked 15% at the Monday open, but the real damage was downstream: refiners in India and South Korea scrambled for alternatives, paying $3–5/bbl premiums on spot cargoes that wouldn't arrive for weeks.

The lesson: the cost of disruption isn't the price of oil. It's the cost of not knowing what to do next.

REROUTE exists to eliminate that latency. We fuse vessel tracking, pipeline telemetry, commodity pricing, and contract data into a single decision surface — and put an AI copilot on top that can reason about your specific supply chain in minutes, not days.

The Crisis

A Chokepoint That Controls
Global Energy Flow

The Strait of Hormuz — just 33km wide at its narrowest — carries one-fifth of the world's oil. When it's disrupted, the cascading effects are immediate and global.

The Chokepoint

Two shipping lanes, each 3km wide, separated by a 3km buffer. Tankers transiting at 12–15 knots have roughly 30 minutes of exposure. Iran's coastline runs along the northern edge; Oman's Musandam Peninsula borders the south. At current throughput, a single day's closure removes ~20M barrels from the global supply equation.1

~21% of global petroleum liquids

Price Shock

Brent-Dubai spread widens immediately. JKM LNG spot can double within a week. Asian refinery margins — already thin at $4–6/bbl — go negative. Platts and Argus assessments become unreliable as physical markets seize up.2

Route Fragmentation

The Petroline (East-West) offers 7M bpd to Yanbu — but Yanbu's jetty capacity can only load ~4M bpd of tankers simultaneously. ADCOP to Fujairah bypasses Hormuz entirely but maxes at 1.8M bpd. The arithmetic doesn't close.3

Contract Cascade

FOB Ras Tanura contracts invoke force majeure. CIF buyers scramble for DES alternatives. Demurrage claims stack up at $40,000–80,000/day per VLCC. War risk insurance premiums jump from 0.05% to 3%+ of hull value overnight.

The 72-Hour Window

IEA member states hold ~1.2 billion barrels in strategic reserves — roughly 90 days at normal draw. But SPR releases take 13–15 days to reach the market. The gap between disruption and relief is where fortunes are made or lost.

What most analyses miss: the second-order effects

01

Fleet redistribution

VLCCs rerouting via Cape of Good Hope need 30% more voyage days. This effectively removes ~15% of global tanker capacity from the market — causing freight rates to spike even for non-Hormuz routes.

02

Refinery yield mismatch

Asian refineries optimized for Arab Medium/Heavy crude can't simply switch to West African or North Sea light sweet grades. Reconfiguring catalytic crackers takes 2–4 weeks and reduces throughput by 10–20%.

03

Petrochemical feedstock crisis

Qatar supplies ~30% of global helium and significant ethane/propane feedstock. A Hormuz closure doesn't just affect fuel — it disrupts semiconductor manufacturing, medical equipment, and plastics production globally.

04

Cascading insurance failure

Lloyd's market war risk pools have finite capacity. Once claims exceed pool limits, P&I clubs withdraw coverage entirely. Vessels become uninsurable. Trade stops not because routes are blocked, but because no one will underwrite the voyage.
